Inner Product Spaces

Inner products, Gram–Schmidt, adjoints, normal and unitary operators, the spectral theorem, the SVD, and quadratic forms.

6.1 Inner Products and Norms

Definition 6.1 (Inner Product) Let \(V\) be a vector space over \(F\). An inner product on \(V\) is a function that assigns, to every ordered pair of vectors \(x\) and \(y\) in \(V\), a scalar in \(F\), denoted \(\langle x, y\rangle\), such that for all \(x, y, z \in V\) and all \(c \in F\), the following hold:

\(\text{(a)}\quad \langle x + z, y\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle + \langle z, y\rangle.\)

\(\text{(b)}\quad \langle c x, y\rangle = c \langle x, y\rangle.\)

\(\text{(c)}\quad \overline{\langle x, y\rangle} = \langle y, x\rangle\) where the bar denotes complex conjugation.

\(\text{(d)}\quad \langle x, x\rangle > 0 \quad \text{if } x \ne 0.\)

  • We assume that all vector spaces are over the field \(F\), where \(F\) denotes either \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\).
  • Note that (c) reduces to \(\langle x, y\rangle = \langle y, x\rangle\) if \(F = \mathbb{R}\).

Theorem 6.1 (Inner Product Properties) Let \(V\) be an inner product space. Then for \(x, y, z \in V\) and \(c \in F\), the following statements are true.

\(\text{(a)}\quad \langle x, y + z\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle + \langle x, z\rangle.\)

\(\text{(b)}\quad \langle x, cy\rangle = \overline{c}\,\langle x, y\rangle.\)

\(\text{(c)}\quad \langle x, 0\rangle = \langle 0, x\rangle = 0.\)

\(\text{(d)}\quad \langle x, x\rangle = 0 \quad \text{if and only if} \quad x = 0.\)

\(\text{(e)}\quad\)If \(\langle x, y\rangle = \langle x, z\rangle\) for all \(x \in V\), then \(y = z\).

Definition 6.2 (Standard Inner Product) For \(x = (a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n)\) and \(y = (b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_n)\) in \(F^n\), define \[ \langle x, y\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \overline{b_i}. \]

The inner product is called the standard inner product on \(F^n\).

  • When \(F = \mathbb{R}\) the conjugations are not needed, and in early courses this standard inner product is usually called the dot product and is denoted by \(x \cdot y\) instead of \(\langle x, y\rangle\).

Definition 6.3 (Norm) Let \(V\) be an inner product space. For \(x \in V\), we define the norm or length of \(x\) by \[ \|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x\rangle}. \]

Theorem 6.2 (Norm Properties) Let \(V\) be an inner product space over \(F\). Then for all \(x, y \in V\) and \(c \in F\), the following statements are true.

\(\text{(a)}\quad \|cx\| = |c| \cdot \|x\|.\)

\(\text{(b)}\quad \|x\| = 0 \quad \text{if and only if} \quad x = 0.\) In any case, \(\|x\| \ge 0\).

\(\text{(c)}\quad\)(Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality) \[ |\langle x, y\rangle| \le \|x\| \cdot \|y\|. \]

\(\text{(d)}\quad\)(Triangle Inequality) \[ \|x + y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|. \]

Definition 6.4 (Conjugate Transpose) Let \(A \in M_{m \times n}(F)\). We define the conjugate transpose or adjoint of \(A\) to be the \(n \times m\) matrix \(A^{*}\) such that \[ (A^{*})_{ij} = \overline{A_{ji}} \quad \text{for all } i, j. \]

Definition 6.5 (Frobenius Inner Product) Let \(V = M_{n \times n}(F)\), and define \[ \langle A, B\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(B^{*} A) \]

for \(A, B \in V\).

The inner product on \(M_{n \times n}(F)\) in this example is called the Frobenius inner product.

Definition 6.6 (Inner Product Space) A vector space \(V\) over \(F\) endowed with a specific inner product is called an inner product space. If \(F = \mathbb{C}\), we call \(V\) a complex inner product space, whereas if \(F = \mathbb{R}\), we call \(V\) a real inner product space.

Definition 6.7 (Orthogonal and Orthonormal) Let \(V\) be an inner product space. Vectors \(x\) and \(y\) in \(V\) are orthogonal (perpendicular) if \(\langle x, y\rangle = 0\).

A subset \(S\) of \(V\) is orthogonal if any two distinct vectors in \(S\) are orthogonal.

  • A vector \(x\) in \(V\) is a unit vector if \(\|x\| = 1\).

Finally, a subset \(S\) of \(V\) is orthonormal if \(S\) is orthogonal and consists entirely of unit vectors.

  • Note that if \(S = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_r\}\) is orthonormal, then \(\langle v_i, v_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}\), where \(\delta_{ij}\) denotes the Kronecker delta.

Definition 6.8 (Normalizing) If \(x\) is any nonzero vector, then \[ \frac{1}{\|x\|} x \] is a unit vector. The process of multiplying a nonzero vector by the reciprocal of its length is called normalizing.

6.2 The Gram–Schmidt Orthogonalization Process and Orthogonal Complements

Definition 6.9 (Orthonormal Basis) Let \(V\) be an inner product space. A subset of \(V\) is an orthonormal basis for \(V\) if it is an ordered basis that is orthonormal.

Theorem 6.3 (Coefficients in an Orthogonal Set) Let \(V\) be an inner product space and \(S = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\}\) be an orthogonal subset of \(V\) consisting of nonzero vectors. If \(y \in \operatorname{span}(S)\), then \[ y = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\langle y, v_i\rangle}{\|v_i\|^2}\, v_i. \]

Corollary 6.1 (Orthogonal Sets Are Independent) Let \(V\) be an inner product space, and let \(S\) be an orthogonal subset of \(V\)

consisting of nonzero vectors. Then \(S\) is linearly independent.

Proposition 6.1 (Gram–Schmidt Process) Let \(V\) be an inner product space and \(S = \{w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n\}\) be a linearly independent subset of \(V\). Define \(S' = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n\}\), where \(v_1 = w_1\) and \[ v_k = w_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \frac{\langle w_k, v_j\rangle}{\|v_j\|^2} \, v_j \quad \text{for } 2 \le k \le n. \]

Then \(S'\) is an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors such that \(\operatorname{span}(S') = \operatorname{span}(S)\).

Definition 6.10 (Orthogonal Complement) Let \(S\) be a nonempty subset of an inner product space \(V\). We define \(S^{\perp}\) (read “\(S\) perp”) to be the set of all vectors in \(V\) that are orthogonal to every vector in \(S\); that is,

\[ S^{\perp} = \{ x \in V : \langle x, y\rangle = 0 \text{ for all } y \in S \}. \]

The set \(S^{\perp}\) is called the orthogonal complement of \(S\).

  • It is easily seen that \(S^{\perp}\) is a subspace of \(V\) for any subset \(S\) of \(V\).

Theorem 6.4 (Existence of Orthonormal Bases) Let \(V\) be a nonzero finite-dimensional inner product space. Then \(V\) has an orthonormal basis \(\beta\). Furthermore, if \(\beta = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n\}\) and \(x \in V\), then \[ x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \langle x, v_i\rangle \, v_i. \]

Corollary 6.2 (Matrix in an Orthonormal Basis) Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional inner product space with an orthonormal basis \(\beta = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n\}\). Let \(T\) be a linear operator on \(V\), and let \(A = [T]_{\beta}\). Then for any \(i\) and \(j\), \[ A_{ij} = \langle T(v_j), v_i \rangle . \]

Theorem 6.5 (Orthogonal Decomposition) Let \(W\) be a finite-dimensional subspace of an inner product space \(V\), and let \(y \in V\). Then there exist unique vectors \(u \in W\) and \(z \in W^{\perp}\) such that \(y = u + z\). Furthermore, if \(\{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\}\) is an orthonormal basis for \(W\), then \[ u = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \langle y, v_i\rangle \, v_i. \]

Corollary 6.3 (Closest Vector Property) The vector \(u\) is the unique vector in \(W\)

that is “closest” to \(y\); that is, for any \(x \in W\),

\[ \|y - x\| \ge \|y - u\|, \]

and this inequality is an equality if and only if \(x = u\).

  • The vector \(u\) in the corollary is called the orthogonal projection of \(y\) on \(W\).

Theorem 6.6 (Orthonormal Extension and Complement) Suppose that \(S = \{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\}\) is an orthonormal set in an \(n\)-dimensional inner product space \(V\). Then

\(\text{(a)}\quad S\) can be extended to an orthonormal basis \(\{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k, v_{k+1}, \ldots, v_n\}\) for \(V\).

\(\text{(b)}\quad\)If \(W = \operatorname{span}(S)\), then \(S_1 = \{v_{k+1}, v_{k+2}, \ldots, v_n\}\) is an orthonormal basis for \(W^{\perp}\) .

\(\text{(c)}\quad\)If \(W\) is any subspace of \(V\), then \[ \dim(V) = \dim(W) + \dim(W^{\perp}). \]

6.3 The Adjoint of a Linear Operator

Theorem 6.7 (Riesz Representation) Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional inner product space over \(\mathbb{F}\), and let \(g : V \to \mathbb{F}\) be a linear transformation. Then there exists a unique vector \(y \in V\) such that \[ g(x) = \langle x, y \rangle \quad \text{for all } x \in V. \]

Definition 6.11 (Adjoint) Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional inner product space, and let \(T\) be a linear operator on \(V\). Then there exists a unique function \(T^* : V \to V\) such that \[ \langle T(x), y \rangle = \langle x, T^*(y) \rangle \quad \text{for all } x, y \in V. \] Furthermore, \(T^*\) is linear. The linear operator \(T^*\) is called the adjoint of the operator \(T\).

Theorem 6.8 (Matrix of the Adjoint) Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional inner product space, and let \(\beta\) be an orthonormal basis for \(V\). If \(T\) is a linear operator on \(V\), then \[ [T^*]_{\beta} = [T]_{\beta}^*. \]

Theorem 6.9 (Adjoint Properties) Let \(V\) be an inner product space, and let \(T\) and \(U\) be linear operators on \(V\). Then:

\(\text{(a)}\quad (T + U)^* = T^* + U^*\);

\(\text{(b)}\quad (cT)^* = \overline{c}\, T^*\) for any \(c \in \mathbb{F}\);

\(\text{(c)}\quad(TU)^* = U^* T^*\);

\(\text{(d)}\quad T^{**} = T\);

\(\text{(e)}\quad I^* = I\).

Corollary 6.4 (Conjugate Transpose Properties) Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n \times n\) matrices. Then:

\(\text{(a)}\quad(A + B)^* = A^* + B^*\);

\(\text{(b)}\quad(cA)^* = \overline{c}\, A^*\) for all \(c \in \mathbb{F}\);

\(\text{(c)}\quad(AB)^* = B^* A^*\);

\(\text{(d)}\quad A^{**} = A\);

\(\text{(e)}\quad I^* = I\).

Definition 6.12 (Least Squares Problem) The problem reduces to finding a vector \(x_0 \in \mathbb{F}^n\) such that \[ \|y - A x_0\| \le \|y - A x\| \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{F}^n. \]

That is, \(Ax_0\) is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the column space of \(A\).

Lemma 6.1 (Adjoint Identity for Matrices) Let \(A \in M_{m \times n}(F)\), \(x \in F^n\), and \(y \in F^m\). Then \[ \langle Ax, y \rangle_m = \langle x, A^* y \rangle_n. \]

Lemma 6.2 (Rank of the Normal Matrix) Let \(A \in M_{m \times n}(F)\). Then \[ \operatorname{rank}(A^*A) = \operatorname{rank}(A). \]

Theorem 6.10 (Least Squares) Let \(A \in M_{m \times n}(F)\) and \(y \in F^m\). Then there exists \(x_0 \in F^n\) such that \[ (A^*A)x_0 = A^*y \] and \[ \|Ax_0 - y\| \le \|Ax - y\| \quad \text{for all } x \in F^n. \]

Furthermore, if \(\operatorname{rank}(A) = n\), then \[ x_0 = (A^*A)^{-1}A^*y. \]

Definition 6.13 (Minimal Solutions to Systems of Linear Equations) Even when a system of linear equations \(Ax = b\) is consistent, there may be no unique solution. In such cases, it may be desirable to find a solution of minimal norm.

A solution \(s\) to \(Ax = b\) is called a minimal solution if \[ \|s\| \le \|u\| \] for all other solutions \(u\).

Theorem 6.11 (Minimal Solutions) Let \(A \in M_{m \times n}(F)\) and \(b \in F^m\). Suppose that \(Ax = b\) is consistent. Then the following statements are true.

\(\text{(a)}\quad\)There exists exactly one minimal solution \(s\) of \(Ax = b\), and \[ s \in R(L_{A^*}). \]

\(\text{(b)}\quad\)The vector \(s\) is the only solution to \(Ax = b\) that lies in \(R(L_{A^*})\); that is, if \(u\) satisfies \[ (AA^*)u = b, \] then \[ s = A^*u. \]

6.4 Normal and Self-Adjoint Operators

Lemma 6.3 (Eigenvectors of the Adjoint) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\). If \(T\) has an eigenvector, then so does \(T^*\).

Theorem 6.12 (Schur) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\).Suppose that the characteristic polynomial of \(T\) splits. Then there exists an orthonormal basis \(\beta\) for \(V\) such that the matrix \([T]_\beta\) is upper triangular.

Definition 6.14 (Normal) Let \(V\) be an inner product space, and let \(T\) be a linear operator on \(V\). We say that \(T\) is normal if \(TT^* = T^*T\). An \(n \times n\) real or complex matrix \(A\) is normal if \(AA^* = A^*A\).

Theorem 6.13 (Normal Operator Properties) Let \(V\) be an inner product space, and let \(T\) be a normal operator on \(V\). Then the following statements are true.

\(\text{(a)}\quad \|T(x)\| = \|T^*(x)\|\) for all \(x \in V\).

\(\text{(b)}\quad T - cI\) is normal for every \(c \in F\).

\(\text{(c)}\quad\)If \(x\) is an eigenvector of \(T\), then \(x\) is also an eigenvector of \(T^*\). In fact, if \(T(x) = \lambda x\), then \(T^*(x) = \overline{\lambda} x\).

\(\text{(d)}\quad\)If \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) are distinct eigenvalues of \(T\) with corresponding eigenvectors \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), then \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are orthogonal.

Theorem 6.14 (Normal Operators and Eigenbases) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space \(V\). Then \(T\) is normal if and only if there exists an orthonormal basis for \(V\) consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\).

Definition 6.15 (Self-Adjoint) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on an inner product space \(V\).We say that \(T\) is self-adjoint (Hermitian) if \(T = T^*\). An \(n \times n\) real or complex matrix \(A\) is self-adjoint (Hermitian) if \(A = A^*\).

Theorem 6.15 (Self-Adjoint Operator Properties) Let \(T\) be a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\). Then

\(\text{(a)}\quad\)Every eigenvalue of \(T\) is real.

\(\text{(b)}\quad\)Suppose that \(V\) is a real inner product space. Then the characteristic polynomial of \(T\) splits.

Theorem 6.16 (Self-Adjoint Operators and Eigenbases) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional real inner product space \(V\). Then \(T\) is self-adjoint if and only if there exists an orthonormal basis \(\beta\) for \(V\) consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\).

6.5 Unitary and Orthogonal Operators and Their Matrices

Definition 6.16 (Unitary and Orthogonal Operator) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\) over \(F\). If \(\|T(x)\| = \|x\|\) for all \(x \in V\), we call \(T\) a unitary operator if \(F = \mathbb{C}\) and an orthogonal operator if \(F = \mathbb{R}\).

Lemma 6.4 (Self-Adjoint Operators with Zero Form) Let \(U\) be a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\). If \(\langle x, U(x) \rangle = 0\) for all \(x \in V\), then \(U = T_0\).

Theorem 6.17 (Characterizations of Unitary Operators) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\). Then the following statements are equivalent.

\(\text{(a)}\quad TT^* = T^*T = I\).

\(\text{(b)}\quad\langle T(x), T(y) \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle\) for all \(x, y \in V\).

\(\text{(c)}\quad\)If \(\beta\) is an orthonormal basis for \(V\), then \(T(\beta)\) is an orthonormal basis for \(V\).

\(\text{(d)}\quad\)There exists an orthonormal basis \(\beta\) for \(V\) such that \(T(\beta)\) is an orthonormal basis for \(V\).

\(\text{(e)}\quad\|T(x)\| = \|x\|\) for all \(x \in V\).

Theorem 6.18 (Orthogonal Eigenbases (Real)) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional real inner product space \(V\). Then \(V\) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of \(T\) with corresponding eigenvalues of absolute value \(1\) if and only if \(T\) is both self-adjoint and orthogonal.

Theorem 6.19 (Orthogonal Eigenbases (Complex)) Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space \(V\). Then \(V\) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of \(T\) with corresponding eigenvalues of absolute value \(1\) if and only if \(T\) is unitary.

Definition 6.17 (Reflection) Let \(L\) be a one-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^2\). We may view \(L\) as a line in the plane through the origin. A linear operator \(T\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is called a reflection of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) about \(L\) if \[ T(x)=x \quad \text{for all } x\in L \] and \[ T(x)=-x \quad \text{for all } x\in L^\perp. \]

Definition 6.18 (Orthogonal and Unitary Matrix) A square matrix \(A\) is called an orthogonal matrix if \[ A^t A = A A^t = I, \] and unitary if \[ A^* A = A A^* = I. \]

Definition 6.19 (Unitary and Orthogonal Equivalence) \(A\) and \(B\) are unitarily equivalent [orthogonally equivalent] if and only if there exists a unitary [orthogonal] matrix \(P\) such that \(A = P^*BP\).

Theorem 6.20 (Normal Matrices and Unitary Equivalence) Let \(A\) be a complex \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is normal if and only if \(A\) is unitarily equivalent to a diagonal matrix.

Theorem 6.21 (Symmetric Matrices and Orthogonal Equivalence) Let \(A\) be a real \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is symmetric (self-adjoint) if and only if \(A\) is orthogonally equivalent to a real diagonal matrix.

6.6 Orthogonal Projections and the Spectral Theorem

Definition 6.20 (Orthogonal Projection) If \(V = W_1 \oplus W_2\), then a linear operator \(T\) on \(V\) is the projection on \(W_1\) along \(W_2\) if, whenever \[ x = x_1 + x_2, \] with \(x_1 \in W_1\) and \(x_2 \in W_2\), we have \[ T(x) = x_1. \]

Let \(V\) be an inner product space, and let \(T: V \to V\) be a projection. We say that \(T\) is an orthogonal projection if \[ R(T)^\perp = N(T) \] and \[ N(T)^\perp = R(T). \]

Theorem 6.22 (Characterization of Orthogonal Projections) Let \(V\) be an inner product space, and let \(T\) be a linear operator on \(V\). Then \(T\) is an orthogonal projection if and only if \(T\) has an adjoint \(T^*\) and \[ T^2 = T = T^*. \]

Theorem 6.23 (The Spectral Theorem) Suppose that \(T\) is a linear operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\) over \(\mathbb{F}\) with the distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_k\). Assume that \(T\) is normal if \(\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}\) and that \(T\) is self-adjoint if \(\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{R}\). For each \(i\) \((1 \le i \le k)\), let \(W_i\) be the eigenspace of \(T\) corresponding to the eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), and let \(T_i\) be the orthogonal projection of \(V\) on \(W_i\). Then the following statements are true.

\(\text{(a)}\quad\) \[ V = W_1 \oplus W_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus W_k. \]

\(\text{(b)}\quad\)If \(W_i'\) denotes the direct sum of the subspaces \(W_j\) for \(j \ne i\), then \[ W_i^\perp = W_i'. \]

\(\text{(c)}\quad\) \[ T_i T_j = \delta_{ij} T_i \quad \text{for } 1 \le i, j \le k. \]

\(\text{(d)}\quad\) \[ I = T_1 + T_2 + \cdots + T_k. \]

\(\text{(e)}\quad\) \[ T = \lambda_1 T_1 + \lambda_2 T_2 + \cdots + \lambda_k T_k. \]

Corollary 6.5 (Normal Operators via the Adjoint) If \(\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}\), then \(T\) is normal if and only if \[ T^* = g(T) \] for some polynomial \(g\).

Corollary 6.6 (Unitary Operators via Eigenvalues) If \(\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}\), then \(T\) is unitary if and only if \(T\) is normal and \[ |\lambda| = 1 \] for every eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(T\).

Corollary 6.7 (Self-Adjoint Operators via Eigenvalues) If \(\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}\) and \(T\) is normal, then \(T\) is self-adjoint if and only if every eigenvalue of \(T\) is real.

Corollary 6.8 (Spectral Projections) Let \(T\) be as in the spectral theorem with spectral decomposition \[ T = \lambda_1 T_1 + \lambda_2 T_2 + \cdots + \lambda_k T_k. \] Then each \(T_j\) is a polynomial in \(T\).

6.7 The Singular Value Decomposition

6.7.1 Singular Value Decomposition

Theorem 6.24 (Singular Value Theorem for Linear Transformations) Let \(V\) and \(W\) be finite-dimensional inner product spaces, and let \(T : V \to W\) be a linear transformation of rank \(r\). Then there exist orthonormal bases \(\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}\) for \(V\) and \(\{u_1, u_2, \dots, u_r\}\) for \(W\) and positive scalars \[ \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r \]

such that

\[ T(v_i) = \begin{cases} \sigma_i u_i, & 1 \le i \le r, \\ 0, & i > r. \end{cases} \]

Thus \[ T^*(u_i) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \langle T^*(u_i), v_j \rangle v_j = \begin{cases} \sigma_i v_i, & i = j \le r, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

  • Conversely, suppose that the preceding conditions are satisfied. Then for \(1 \le i \le n\), \(v_i\) is an eigenvector of \(T^*T\) with corresponding eigenvalue \(\sigma_i^2\) if \(1 \le i \le r\), and \(0\) if \(i > r\). Therefore the scalars \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_r\) are uniquely determined by \(T\).

  • Each \(v_i\) is an eigenvector of \(T^*T\) with corresponding eigenvalue \(\sigma_i^2\) if \(i \le r\), and \(0\) if \(i > r\).

Definition 6.21 (Singular Values of a Linear Transformation) The unique scalars \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_r\) are called the singular values of \(T\). If \(r\) is less than both \(m\) and \(n\), then the term singular value is extended to include \(\sigma_{r+1} = \cdots = \sigma_k = 0\), where \(k\) is the minimum of \(m\) and \(n\).

Definition 6.22 (Singular Values of a Matrix) Let \(A\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix. We define the singular values of \(A\) to be the singular values of the linear transformation \(L_A\).

Theorem 6.25 (Singular Value Decomposition Theorem for Matrices) Let \(A\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix of rank \(r\) with the positive singular values \[ \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r, \] and let \(\Sigma\) be the \(m \times n\) matrix defined by \[ \Sigma_{ij} = \begin{cases} \sigma_i, & i = j \le r, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Then there exists an \(m \times m\) unitary matrix \(U\) and an \(n \times n\) unitary matrix \(V\) such that \[ A = U \Sigma V^* . \]

Definition 6.23 (Singular Value Decomposition of a Matrix) Let \(A\) be an \(m \times n\) matrix of rank \(r\) with positive singula values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r\). A factorization \(A = U \Sigma V^*\) where \(U\) and \(V\) are unitary matrices and \(\Sigma\) is the \(m \times n\) matrix is called a singular value decomposition of \(A\).

6.8 Bilinear and Quadratic Forms

Definition 6.24 (Bilinear Form) Let \(V\) be a vector space over a field \(F\). A function \(H\) from the set \(V \times V\) of ordered pairs of vectors to \(F\) is called a bilinear form on \(V\) if \(H\) is linear in each variable when the other variable is held fixed; that is, \(H\) is a bilinear form on \(V\) if

\(\text{(a)}\quad H(ax_1 + x_2, y) = aH(x_1, y) + H(x_2, y)\) for all \(x_1, x_2, y \in V\) and \(a \in F\),

\(\text{(b)}\quad H(x, ay_1 + y_2) = aH(x, y_1) + H(x, y_2)\) for all \(x, y_1, y_2 \in V\) and \(a \in F\).

Definition 6.25 (Sum and Scalar Multiple of Bilinear Forms) Let \(V\) be a vector space, let \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) be bilinear forms on \(V\), and let \(a\) be a scalar. We define the sum \(H_1 + H_2\) and the scalar product \(aH_1\) by the equations \[ (H_1 + H_2)(x,y) = H_1(x,y) + H_2(x,y) \]

and

\[ (aH_1)(x,y) = a(H_1(x,y)), \quad \text{for all } x,y \in V. \]

  • For any vector space \(V\), the sum of two bilinear forms and the product of a scalar and a bilinear form on \(V\) are again bilinear forms on \(V\). Furthermore, \(\mathcal{B}(V)\) is a vector space with respect to these operations.

Definition 6.26 (Matrix Representation of a Bilinear Form) Let \(\beta = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}\) be an ordered basis for an \(n\)-dimensional vector space \(V\), and let \(H \in \mathcal{B}(V)\). We can associate with \(H\) an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) whose entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is defined by \[ A_{ij} = H(v_i, v_j), \quad \text{for } i,j = 1,2,\dots,n. \] The matrix \(A\) above is called the matrix representation of \(H\) with respect to the ordered basis \(\beta\) and is denoted by \(\psi_\beta(H)\).

Theorem 6.26 (Bilinear Forms as Matrices) Let \(F\) be a field, \(n\) a positive integer, and \(\beta\) be the standard ordered basis for \(F^n\). Then for any \(H \in \mathcal{B}(F^n)\), there exists a unique matrix \(A \in M_{n \times n}(F)\), namely \(A = \psi_\beta(H)\), such that \[ H(x,y) = x^t A y \quad \text{for all } x,y \in F^n. \]

Definition 6.27 (Congruent Matrices) Let \(A, B \in M_{n \times n}(F)\). Then \(B\) is said to be congruent to \(A\) if there exists an invertible matrix \(Q \in M_{n \times n}(F)\) such that \[ B = Q^t A Q. \]

Theorem 6.27 (Change of Basis for Bilinear Forms) Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space with ordered bases \(\beta = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}\) and \(\gamma = \{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n\}\), and let \(Q\) be the change-of-coordinate matrix changing \(\gamma\)-coordinates into \(\beta\)-coordinates. Then, for any \(H \in \mathcal{B}(V)\), we have \[ \psi_\gamma(H) = Q^t \psi_\beta(H) Q. \] Therefore \(\psi_\gamma(H)\) is congruent to \(\psi_\beta(H)\).

Definition 6.28 (Symmetric Bilinear Form) A bilinear form \(H\) on a vector space \(V\) is symmetric if \(H(x,y) = H(y,x)\) for all \(x,y \in V\). As the name suggests, symmetric bilinear forms correspond to symmetric matrices.

Theorem 6.28 (Symmetric Forms and Symmetric Matrices) Let \(H\) be a bilinear form on a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\), and let \(\beta\) be an ordered basis for \(V\). Then \(H\) is symmetric if and only if \(\psi_\beta(H)\) is symmetric.

Definition 6.29 (Diagonalizable Bilinear Form) A bilinear form \(H\) on a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\) is called diagonalizable if there is an ordered basis \(\beta\) for \(V\) such that \(\psi_\beta(H)\) is a diagonal matrix.

Lemma 6.5 (Nonzero Values of Symmetric Forms) Let \(H\) be a nonzero symmetric bilinear form on a vector space \(V\) over a field \(F\) not of characteristic two. Then there is a vector \(x\) in \(V\) such that \(H(x,x) \neq 0\).

Theorem 6.29 (Diagonalization of Symmetric Forms) Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field \(F\) not of characteristic two. Then every symmetric bilinear form on \(V\) is diagonalizable.

Definition 6.30 (Quadratic Form) Let \(V\) be a vector space over \(F\). A function \(K : V \to F\) is called a quadratic form if there exists a symmetric bilinear form \(H \in \mathcal{B}(V)\) such that \[ K(x) = H(x,x) \quad \text{for all } x \in V. \]

Definition 6.31 (Quadratic Form as a Polynomial) Given the variables \(t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n\) that take values in a field \(F\) not of characteristic two and given (not necessarily distinct) scalars \(a_{ij}\) \((1 \le i \le j \le n)\), define the polynomial \[ f(t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n) = \sum_{i \le j} a_{ij} t_i t_j . \]

Any such polynomial is a quadratic form. In fact, if \(\beta\) is the standard ordered basis for \(F^n\), then the symmetric bilinear form \(H\) corresponding to the quadratic form \(f\) has the matrix representation \(\psi_\beta(H) = A\), where \[ A_{ij} = A_{ji} = \begin{cases} a_{ii}, & \text{if } i = j, \\ \frac{1}{2} a_{ij}, & \text{if } i \ne j . \end{cases} \]

Theorem 6.30 (Principal Axes of a Quadratic Form) Let \(K\) be a quadratic form on a finite-dimensional real inner product space \(V\). There exists an orthonormal basis \(\beta = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}\) for \(V\) and scalars \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n\) (not necessarily distinct) such that if \(x \in V\) and \[ x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i v_i, \quad s_i \in \mathbb{R}, \]

then

\[ K(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i s_i^2. \]
