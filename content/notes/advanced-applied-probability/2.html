Laws of Large Numbers

2.1 Independence

2.1.1 Basic Definitions

Definition 2.1 (Independent Events) Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B). \]

Definition 2.2 (Independent Random Variables) Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be random variables defined on the same probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\):

\[ X,Y:(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal R). \]

They are independent if, for all \(C,D\in\mathcal R\),

\[ P(X\in C,Y\in D)=P(X\in C)P(Y\in D). \]

That is, the events \(A=\{X\in C\}\) and \(B=\{Y\in D\}\) are independent.

Definition 2.3 (Independent σ-Fields) Let \((\Omega,\mathcal H,P)\) be a probability space, and let \(\mathcal F,\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H\) be two sub-\(\sigma\)-fields. They are independent if

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B), \qquad\forall A\in\mathcal F,\ \forall B\in\mathcal G. \]

That is, every event in \(\mathcal F\) is independent of every event in \(\mathcal G\).

Theorem 2.1 (Independence and Generated σ-Fields) Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be random variables on the same probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal H,P)\).

  1. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, then \(\sigma(X)\) and \(\sigma(Y)\) are independent.

  2. Conversely, if \(\mathcal F,\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H\) are independent sub-\(\sigma\)-fields, \(X\) is \(\mathcal F\)-measurable, and \(Y\) is \(\mathcal G\)-measurable, then \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.

2.1.2 Independence of Finite Families

Work on a common probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal H,P)\).

Definition 2.4 (Independence of Finitely Many σ-Fields) The sub-\(\sigma\)-fields \(\mathcal F_1,\ldots,\mathcal F_n\subseteq\mathcal H\) are independent if, whenever \(A_i\in\mathcal F_i\) for \(i=1,\ldots,n\),

\[ P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i\right)=\prod_{i=1}^n P(A_i). \]

Definition 2.5 (Independence of Finitely Many Random Variables) The random variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are independent if, whenever \(B_i\in\mathcal R\) for \(i=1,\ldots,n\),

\[ P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n\{X_i\in B_i\}\right) =\prod_{i=1}^n P(X_i\in B_i). \]

Definition 2.6 (Independence of Finitely Many Events) The events \(A_1,\ldots,A_n\in\mathcal H\) are independent if, for every nonempty subset \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\),

\[ P\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\right)=\prod_{i\in I}P(A_i). \]

2.1.3 Joint Distributions

Theorem 2.2 (Joint Distribution of Independent Random Variables) Suppose \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are independent random variables and \(X_i\) has distribution \(\mu_i\) for \(i=1,\ldots,n\). Then \((X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) has distribution

\[ \mu_1\times\cdots\times\mu_n. \]

2.1.4 Expectations

Theorem 2.3 (Expectations of Functions of Independent Random Variables) Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent and have distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). Let \(h:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be measurable. If either

  1. \(h\ge0\), or

  2. \(E|h(X,Y)|<\infty\),

then

\[ E[h(X,Y)]=\iint_{\mathbb R^2}h(x,y)\,\mu(dx)\,\nu(dy). \]

In particular, let \(h(x,y)=f(x)g(y)\), where \(f,g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) are measurable functions. If either

  1. \(f,g\ge0\), or

  2. both \(E|f(X)|<\infty\) and \(E|g(Y)|<\infty\),

then

\[ E[f(X)g(Y)]=E[f(X)]\cdot E[g(Y)]. \]

Theorem 2.4 (Expectation of a Product of Independent Random Variables) Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent random variables. If either

  1. \(X_i\ge0\) for all \(i\), or

  2. \(E|X_i|<\infty\) for all \(i\),

then

\[ E\left[\prod_{i=1}^n X_i\right]=\prod_{i=1}^n EX_i. \]

2.1.5 Sums and Convolution

Theorem 2.5 (Distribution of the Sum of Independent Random Variables) If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\), and \(G(y)=P(Y\le y)\), then

\[ P(X+Y\le z)=\int_{\mathbb R}F(z-y)\,dG(y). \]

2.1.5.1 Convolution

The integral on the right-hand side is called the convolution of \(F\) and \(G\) and is denoted by \((F*G)(z)\).

Here \(dG(y)\) denotes integration with respect to the distribution measure of \(Y\).

2.2 Conditional Expectation

Definition 2.7 (Conditional Expectation) Given a probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal F_0,P)\), a sub-\(\sigma\)-field \(\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal F_0\), and an \(\mathcal F_0\)-measurable random variable \(X\) with \(E|X|<\infty\), the conditional expectation of \(X\) given \(\mathcal F\), denoted \(E(X\mid\mathcal F)\), is any random variable \(Y\) satisfying:

  1. \(Y\) is \(\mathcal F\)-measurable.

  2. For all \(A\in\mathcal F\),

    \[ \int_A X\,dP=\int_A Y\,dP. \]

Any \(Y\) satisfying (i) and (ii) is called a version of \(E(X\mid\mathcal F)\).

NoteExample: Conditioning on a Random Variable

Let \(E|X|<\infty\) and \(\mathcal F=\sigma(Z)\). Then

\[ Y=E[X\mid\mathcal F]=E[X\mid Z]=g(Z)\quad\text{a.s.} \]

Here \(Y\) is an \(\mathcal F\)-measurable random variable, and \(g\) is measurable. Pointwise,

\[ Y(\omega)=g(Z(\omega))\quad\text{for almost every }\omega. \]

Thus, \(Y\) depends only on \(Z\); observing \(Z=z\) determines its value \(g(z)\).

NoteConditional Expectation as Optimal Prediction

If \(E[X^2]<\infty\), \(Y=E[X\mid Z]\) is the best predictor of \(X\) based on \(Z\) under squared loss:

\[ E[(X-Y)^2]=\min_{h\text{ measurable}}E[(X-h(Z))^2]. \]

In regression, \(Z\) contains the input features and \(X\) is the target; training with squared loss approximates \(E[X\mid Z]\) within the chosen model class.

2.2.1 Regular Conditional Probabilities

Definition 2.8 (Regular Conditional Distribution) Let \((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\) be a probability space, \(X:(\Omega,\mathcal F)\to(S,\mathcal S)\) a measurable map, and \(\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F\) a sub-\(\sigma\)-field. A map \(\mu:\Omega\times\mathcal S\to[0,1]\) is a regular conditional distribution for \(X\) given \(\mathcal G\) if:

  1. For each \(A\in\mathcal S\), \(\omega\mapsto\mu(\omega,A)\) is a version of \(P(X\in A\mid\mathcal G)\).

  2. For almost every \(\omega\), \(A\mapsto\mu(\omega,A)\) is a probability measure on \((S,\mathcal S)\).

When \(S=\Omega\) and \(X\) is the identity map, \(\mu\) is called a regular conditional probability.

NoteExample: Conditional Distribution from a Joint Density

Suppose \(X,Y\) have a joint density \(f(x,y)>0\). For Borel \(A\subseteq\mathbb R\), define

\[ \mu(y,A)=\frac{\displaystyle\int_A f(x,y)\,dx} {\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb R}f(x,y)\,dx}. \]

Where the denominator is not finite and positive, choose any fixed probability distribution instead.

Then \((\omega,A)\mapsto\mu(Y(\omega),A)\) is a regular conditional distribution of \(X\) given \(\sigma(Y)\): \(\mu(y,\cdot)\) gives the distribution of \(X\) after observing \(Y=y\).

2.3 Laws of Large Numbers

Throughout this section, \(S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n\).

2.3.1 Weak Laws of Large Numbers

Theorem 2.6 (L² Weak Law) Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be uncorrelated random variables with \(EX_i=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\le C<\infty\). Then, as \(n\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{S_n}{n}\to\mu \quad\text{in } L^2 \text{ and in probability.} \]

NoteWhy the Variance Bound Is Enough

Because the variables are uncorrelated, the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances:

\[ E\left(\frac{S_n}{n}-\mu\right)^2 =\frac{\operatorname{Var}(S_n)}{n^2} \le\frac{C}{n}\to0. \]

This gives convergence in \(L^2\), and Chebyshev’s inequality then gives convergence in probability.

Theorem 2.7 (Khinchin’s Weak Law) Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be i.i.d. with \(E|X_1|<\infty\) and \(EX_1=\mu\). Then

\[ \frac{S_n}{n}\to\mu \quad\text{in probability.} \]

NoteWhy No Variance Is Needed

The variables may have infinite variance, so Theorem 2.6 does not apply directly. The proof truncates \(X_i\) at level \(n\) and applies the variance argument to the truncated variables. The condition \(E|X_1|<\infty\) makes the truncated part negligible.

2.3.2 Borel-Cantelli Lemmas

Definition 2.9 (Infinitely Often) For events \(A_1,A_2,\ldots\), the event that \(A_n\) occurs infinitely often is

\[ \{A_n\text{ i.o.}\} =\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n =\bigcap_{m=1}^\infty\bigcup_{n=m}^\infty A_n. \]

Lemma 2.1 (First Borel-Cantelli Lemma) If \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n)<\infty\), then

\[ P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=0. \]

Lemma 2.2 (Second Borel-Cantelli Lemma) If the events \(A_1,A_2,\ldots\) are independent and \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n)=\infty\), then

\[ P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=1. \]

NoteWhy Each Lemma Holds

For the first lemma, the union bound gives

\[ P(A_n\text{ i.o.}) \le P\left(\bigcup_{n=m}^\infty A_n\right) \le\sum_{n=m}^\infty P(A_n)\to0 \quad\text{as } m\to\infty. \]

For the second lemma, independence and \(1-x\le e^{-x}\) give

\[ P\left(\bigcap_{n=m}^M A_n^c\right) =\prod_{n=m}^M\bigl(1-P(A_n)\bigr) \le\exp\left(-\sum_{n=m}^M P(A_n)\right)\to0 \quad\text{as } M\to\infty. \]

Independence cannot be dropped. If \(A_n=A\) for all \(n\) and \(0<P(A)<1\), then \(\sum_n P(A_n)=\infty\) but \(P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=P(A)<1\).

2.3.3 Khinchin–Kolmogorov Theorem

Lemma 2.3 (Kolmogorov’s Maximal Inequality) Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent with \(EX_i=0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i)<\infty\). Then, for every \(x>0\),

\[ P\left(\max_{1\le k\le n}|S_k|\ge x\right) \le\frac{\operatorname{Var}(S_n)}{x^2}. \]

Theorem 2.8 (Khinchin–Kolmogorov Convergence Theorem) Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be independent with \(EX_n=0\). If

\[ \sum_{n=1}^\infty\operatorname{Var}(X_n)<\infty, \]

then \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty X_n\) converges almost surely.

NoteWhy the Series Converges

Apply Lemma 2.3 to the terms after index \(M\):

\[ P\left(\max_{M<k\le N}|S_k-S_M|\ge\varepsilon\right) \le\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\sum_{n=M+1}^N\operatorname{Var}(X_n). \]

Let \(N\to\infty\) and then \(M\to\infty\). The right side tends to \(0\), so the partial sums are almost surely a Cauchy sequence.

NoteExample: Random Harmonic Series

Let \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\ldots\) be independent with \(P(\varepsilon_n=\pm1)=1/2\). Then \(\sum_n\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_n/n)=\sum_n 1/n^2<\infty\), so \(\sum_n\varepsilon_n/n\) converges almost surely, even though \(\sum_n 1/n\) diverges.

2.4 Supplementary Notes

2.4.1 Key Results

  • Theorem 2.1: Independent random variables generate independent \(\sigma\)-fields, and variables measurable with respect to independent \(\sigma\)-fields are independent.
  • Theorem 2.2: Independent random variables have the product distribution \(\mu_1\times\cdots\times\mu_n\) as their joint distribution.
  • Theorem 2.3 and Theorem 2.4: Under independence, \(E[f(X)g(Y)]=E[f(X)]\cdot E[g(Y)]\), and expectation factors over products.
  • Theorem 2.5: \(P(X+Y\le z)=\int_{\mathbb R}F(z-y)\,dG(y)\), the convolution of \(F\) and \(G\).
  • Definition 2.7: \(E(X\mid\mathcal F)\) is an \(\mathcal F\)-measurable variable with the same integral as \(X\) over every \(A\in\mathcal F\).
  • Theorem 2.7: If \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) are i.i.d. with \(E|X_1|<\infty\), then \(S_n/n\to EX_1\) in probability.
  • Lemma 2.1 and Lemma 2.2: \(\sum_n P(A_n)<\infty\) gives \(P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=0\); for independent events, \(\sum_n P(A_n)=\infty\) gives \(P(A_n\text{ i.o.})=1\).
  • Theorem 2.8: For independent mean-zero \(X_n\) with \(\sum_n\operatorname{Var}(X_n)<\infty\), the series \(\sum_n X_n\) converges almost surely.
